> TL;DR — Enterprise AI is mostly distribution and trust, not
> modeling. A non-tech company is the most underrated first job for
> an AI engineer: you ship for users who do not care about your
> craft, which is exactly the right calibration. Agents over chat,
> MCP over custom tool wiring, iteration over specs.

## What this is and is not

Sanitized writeup of one year as an AI Engineer at **Beam Suntory**,
inside the AI software team. No internal product names, no real
metrics in absolute terms, no customer names. The patterns are
publicly shareable; the specifics are not. See the
[case study](/projects/suntory-genai) for the engineering side.

## What I have actually been doing

I build end-to-end GenAI solutions across departments. Each one
targets a specific business workflow that someone in the company is
doing manually today. The flow is:

1. Spot a repeated workflow that costs hours per week
2. Architect a solution on Azure (Azure AI Foundry, Azure AI Search,
   sometimes GCP for specific use cases)
3. Build the agent or the MCP-tooled flow with LangChain or the
   OpenAI Agents SDK
4. Get a rough version in the user&apos;s hands within a couple of weeks
5. Iterate with the real user until it becomes part of their day

I have led **5+ distinct use cases** this way. Several have moved from
prototype to "the default tool that team reaches for" within a few
months. The work bleeds across general-purpose agents, MCP tools, and
Azure data use cases.

## What surprised me

**The hard part is distribution, not modeling.** I came in expecting
to optimize retrieval and tune prompts. The bottleneck turned out to
be: how do you put a tool in front of a non-technical employee, in
the workflow they already do every day, in a way they trust enough to
act on?

The model is commoditized. The work that is rare is turning a
competent model into a tool a non-technical user reaches for instead
of asking a colleague.

**Citations matter more than accuracy.** A 95%-accurate answer with
no sources gets less adoption than an 85%-accurate answer with one
clickable source. Trust scales with provenance, not with eval
scores.

**Agents are the right abstraction.** I started on workloads where
LangChain orchestration made sense and have steadily moved toward the
**OpenAI Agents SDK** as the better default. Each solution maps to
one focused agent with curated tools and a clear handoff, not a
generic chatbot. The agent pattern (instructions + tools +
guardrails) ships faster than custom orchestration and survives
provider upgrades better.

**MCP is the standardized tool layer.** Once you have more than one
agent, you do not want to redefine every tool per provider. MCP gives
you a single tool definition that works across providers, makes the
surface area the security team has to review explicit, and keeps
tool definitions version-controlled.

## What this year has taught me

- How to **operate in an enterprise environment** — security review,
  procurement, data-classification handling, change management. None
  of these would come up in a YC startup of seven engineers
- How to **recognize a real business use case** versus a demo:
  workflows that are repeated weekly by multiple people are gold;
  one-off curiosity is not
- How to assemble **end-to-end secure cloud architectures** on Azure
  rather than just calling APIs from a notebook
- How to **iterate fast** under constraints that startups do not have
  and still ship — most of the value of the role comes from this skill

## Why this might be the best first job for an AI engineer

The advice from US Twitter is to join a YC-backed AI startup as a
founding engineer. That is great advice when it is available. It is
not the only good path.

A non-tech Fortune 500 gives you:

- **Real users who do not care about your craft.** They will not give
  you credit for elegant retrieval. They will use whatever ships and
  works, and stop using whatever does not. This is exactly the right
  calibration.
- **A long horizon.** Startups optimize for a six-month story.
  Enterprise AI work optimizes for systems you will operate for
  years. Both are valuable; you cannot learn the second one in the
  first context.
- **Adjacent disciplines.** Security review, data governance, change
  management, internal comms. All of them will matter in any team I
  am on later.

The cost is real: you ship less of the cool stuff, you have less
visibility in the open AI community, and you fight harder for the
ambitious work. Trade-off, not strict downgrade.
