## What surprised me

**The hard part is distribution, not modeling.** I came in expecting to
optimize retrieval and fine-tune embeddings. The bottleneck turned out
to be: how do you put a chat interface in front of an employee who has
never used ChatGPT, in a workflow they already do every day, in under
12 clicks, with an answer they trust enough to act on?

The model is a commodity. The thing that&apos;s rare is the work that turns
a competent model into a tool a non-technical user reaches for instead
of asking a colleague.

**Citations matter more than accuracy.** A 95%-accurate answer with no
sources gets less adoption than an 85%-accurate answer with one
clickable source. Trust scales with provenance, not with eval scores.

**Latency under 3 seconds, or nothing.** I underestimated this. Above
3 seconds, users assume it&apos;s broken. They open a new tab and never
come back. The retrieval pipeline must hit the budget; nothing else
matters until it does.

## What I&apos;d build different next time

**Evaluation harness on day one.** We built ours about three months in.
Before that, every &quot;improvement&quot; was a vibe. Once the harness existed,
the iteration loop became measurable: change → score → keep or revert.
This is the single most leveraged piece of infrastructure on any GenAI
project. Build it first.

**Multi-LLM routing as a default, not an optimization.** Provider
outages happen, prices change, capabilities evolve. The routing layer
is where these conversations get to happen. Building it later means
retrofitting an architecture; building it from the start means you can
upgrade calmly.

**Less framework, more code.** I started with [REQUIRES INPUT: framework
you used — LangChain / LlamaIndex / etc.] for orchestration. Six months
later I would have shipped faster, debugged faster, and shipped more
maintainable code with a 200-line custom orchestrator. Frameworks are
great for prototypes and bad for the system you have to operate for
years.

## Why this might be the best first job for an AI engineer

The advice from US Twitter is to join a YC-backed AI startup as a
founding engineer. That&apos;s great advice when it&apos;s available. It is not
the only good path.

A non-tech company gives you:

- **Real users who don&apos;t care about your craft.** They will not give you
  credit for elegant retrieval. They will use whatever ships and works
  and stop using whatever doesn&apos;t. This is exactly the right calibration.
- **A long horizon.** Startups optimize for a six-month story. Enterprise
  AI work optimizes for a system you&apos;ll operate for years. Both are
  valuable; you can&apos;t learn the second one in the first context.
- **Adjacent disciplines.** I&apos;ve had to learn security review, data
  governance, change management, internal comms. None of these would
  have come up in a YC startup of seven engineers. All of them will
  matter in any team I&apos;m on later.

The cost is: you ship less of the cool stuff, you have less visibility
in the open AI community, and you have to fight harder for the
ambitious work. Trade-off, not strict downgrade.
