> TL;DR — Communities are pull, not push. The members who build are
> worth ten members who show up. Speaker events look impressive and
> matter the least. Plan for the people who come after you.

## Why AISC Madrid exists

Madrid has world-class engineering schools and a fast-growing AI
hiring market, but the two ecosystems were not talking to each
other. There were academic clubs at every university, but none of
them were builder-first and none crossed campus lines. I noticed
because I needed it: I came back from a year at UC Riverside, where
the AISC chapter shaped my exchange, and Madrid had nothing
equivalent.

On **1 September 2025**, we co-founded the first AISC chapter in
Europe and the first AI student association at UC3M. Today we are
**500+ members strong**, run by a core team of **16**, with **30+
events** in our first year and partners including **Microsoft,
GitHub, Hugging Face, and Spotify**.

## The first workshop

I taught the first workshop myself. AI fundamentals — what makes a
model a model, why scale matters, what an embedding is. We expected
maybe 30 people. More than that showed up. We did not fit in the
room. Half the audience watched standing in the corridor through the
open door, and we kept the talk shorter than planned because the air
in the room was finite.

That night told us something about Madrid that I had been
underestimating: the demand for an honest, fundamentals-first AI
conversation was enormous. The students were there. We had been
hiding from them.

## What we got right

**Cold outreach to industry.** I assumed AI engineers and product
people in Madrid would say no to a student club they had never heard
of. Most of them said yes. We have hosted speakers from Microsoft,
GitHub, Hugging Face, and Spotify in our first year. The ones who
said no usually said "not this month, ask me next quarter."

**Demystifying instead of impressing.** Our positioning from day one
was: AI is not magic. We explain the fundamentals, then the actual
tools that the industry uses day-to-day, then bring back alumni and
professionals to share how they got to the companies that are
shaping the field. The combination of "this is reachable" and "here
is exactly the path" is what keeps the community growing.

**Builder workshops, not just talks.** Speaker events fill rooms but
produce nothing. Workshops where members ship something small produce
members who can do things, members who can teach the next cohort, and
a portfolio of work other students recognize.

## What I got wrong

**Trying to do too much in the first semester.** We launched five
parallel initiatives in the first three months and ran every single
one ragged. The follow-up semester I cut us back to three core
formats — workshops, talks, and full-day events — and the quality
went up across the board.

## The hard part nobody mentions

Student leadership turns over every 1-2 years. The hardest thing to
build is the institutional memory and the operating cadence that
survives the people who built it. We are too young to know if we
have succeeded at this. Ask me in 2028.

## Why this matters

Spotter taught me to ship. Suntory taught me to ship at scale. AISC
taught me that the best leverage on the AI ecosystem in a country is
the next wave of builders. Spain produces strong engineering talent
and exports too much of it. AISC is a small contribution to the
inverse — making the local ecosystem worth staying for, even if I
(probably) leave it.

If you are starting one yourself: ship the first event before
forming a team. Default to workshops, ration speaker events. The
community is the members who build, not the members on the mailing
list. And plan for your succession from day one.
