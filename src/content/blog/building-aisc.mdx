> TL;DR — Communities are pull, not push. Don&apos;t recruit a leadership
> team before you have something to lead. Speaker events look impressive
> and matter the least. The members who build are worth ten members who
> show up.

## Why AISC Madrid exists

Madrid has world-class engineering schools, a fast-growing AI hiring
market, and zero student-led AI community connecting the two. There were
academic clubs at every university, but none of them were builder-first
and none of them crossed campus lines. I noticed because I needed it,
specifically: I&apos;d come back from a year at UC Riverside where the AISC
chapter shaped my exchange, and Madrid had nothing like it.

In July 2025, I co-founded the first AISC chapter in Europe and the first
AI student association at UC3M. We&apos;re still small but we&apos;re building
the institution I wished existed when I started college.

## What I got wrong in month one

**Recruiting a board before having a product.** I spent the first three
weeks assembling a leadership team for a community that didn&apos;t exist
yet. The team meetings were elaborate, the actual community work didn&apos;t
move. The fix was inverting it: ship the first event, see who shows up
and wants to help, then form a board from the people who already
showed up.

## What I got right

**Cold outreach to speakers.** I assumed AI researchers and industry
people in Madrid would say no to a student club they had never heard
of. Most of them said yes. [REQUIRES INPUT: name 2-3 speakers or
partner organizations you brought in, if you&apos;re comfortable.] The
ones who said no usually said &quot;not this month, ask me next quarter.&quot;

**Builder workshops over talks.** Speaker events fill rooms but produce
nothing. Workshops where members ship something small — a fine-tune, a
demo, a paper reproduction — produce members who can do things, members
who can teach the next cohort, and a portfolio of work other students
recognize.

## The hard parts nobody mentions

**Continuity.** Student leadership turns over every 1-2 years. The
hardest thing to build is the institutional memory and the operating
cadence that survives the people who built it. We&apos;re too young to
know if we&apos;ve succeeded at this. Ask me in 2028.

**Saying no.** Once a community is visible, every external actor wants
something from it: sponsors with strings attached, courses wanting to
recruit, founders wanting beta testers. Most of these are fine
individually and a disaster collectively. We&apos;ve had to learn to say no
to good offers because the cumulative cost was too high.

## What I&apos;d tell another student trying to start one

1. **Ship the first event before forming a team.** Members find you;
   you do not find members.
2. **Default to builder workshops, ration speaker events.**
3. **The community is the members who build, not the members on the
   mailing list.** Make sure your metrics reflect this.
4. **Plan for your own succession from day one.** The community
   matters more than your founder status in it.

## Why this matters for me

Spain produces strong engineering talent and exports too much of it. AISC
is one small contribution to the inverse — making the local ecosystem
worth staying for, even if I (probably) leave it.
